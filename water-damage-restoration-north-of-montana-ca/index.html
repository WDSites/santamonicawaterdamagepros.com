North of Montana is the residential area on the north side of Santa Monica, above Montana Avenue and running to the ocean bluffs and the northern city limit. Larger houses mean more floor area, more rooms and more finishes that water can reach, and that shapes how a restoration job is planned.

The North of Montana Association describes its area as bounded by the north side of Montana Avenue, the ocean, the northern city limits and 26th Street, with approximately 5,200 homes. That is how the association describes its area, not an official city boundary. Montana Avenue itself is a commercial street and is not covered by this residential page.

Bigger houses, bigger loss paths

Neighborhood reference descriptions say the area has larger family homes on larger lots, with a typical lot of 7,500 square feet. More square footage means more floor, drywall and ceiling for water to reach after a single failure. A leak on an upper floor can run through a ceiling into a room below, so a crew checks every level under the source.

Finishes also change the scope. Where a house has hardwood floors, built-in cabinets or plaster ceilings, drying and repair decisions are made material by material. Crews read the boards and the cabinet bases with a meter and tell you which can be dried and which cannot before work starts.

Storms, bluffs and what was reported

In January 2005, nearly four inches of rain fell at Santa Monica Airport over a few days. City officials reported only minor street flooding, credited the drainage system and said the Palisades Park bluffs were saturated, according to Santa Monica Lookout and Santa Monica Daily Press reports. One lane of Pacific Coast Highway closed after soil washed down from the bluffs.

Those reports do not describe damage to homes north of Montana. Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status is not verified, so check an address on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center. Roof leaks, aging fixtures and appliance failures are more useful to plan for than a mapped flood, and the roof leak water damage page covers the first of these.

Safety first: upper floors and wet ceilings

A ceiling that is bulging or dripping may hold a lot of water, so keep people out from under it. Stay out of water near outlets and light fixtures, and switch off power at the panel only if it is dry and reachable. Do not climb onto a wet roof. Call 911 for gas odors or sparks.

What crews do in a larger home

Extraction comes first, followed by moisture mapping across every level the water touched. Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed from the map, and the readings are logged each visit. A larger house usually needs more equipment and more days, and the crew explains the plan and how the price is set before work begins.

For a broken line, see burst pipe water damage. For a failed tank, see water heater leak cleanup. For a stained ceiling after the source is fixed, see ceiling water damage repair, and for the drying process, see structural drying.

Insurance documentation

Photos of each room, moisture readings and an itemized scope can help describe the loss, especially in a larger home with many finishes. Whether the loss is covered depends on the cause and the policy terms. Ask your insurer which documents it wants, and keep receipts for emergency steps.
